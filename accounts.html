Accounts & Tax

Fill the sheltered space first

Over 30 years, the account wrapper around an investment often matters as much as the investment itself. Every country covered here offers legal, government-designed spaces where compounding runs partly or wholly tax-free. Using them fully — before investing a single unit of currency in an ordinary taxable account — is the closest thing to free return you will ever get.

Which year these figures are for. Everything below is the 2026 tax year — US limits are the IRS figures for 2026, Japan is the NISA and iDeCo system in force in 2026, Korea is the regime after the 2026 ISA expansion. Each section lists its primary source and the date the figures were last checked. They change with almost every budget cycle in all three countries. Verify against official sources (IRS, 国税庁/金融庁, 국세청/금융위원회) or a licensed professional before acting.

US investor priority order (a common default)

  1. 401(k) up to the full employer match.
  2. High-interest debt paid off; emergency fund in cash.
  3. Roth or traditional IRA to the limit.
  4. HSA to the limit, if eligible.
  5. Rest of 401(k) to the limit.
  6. Taxable brokerage with broad index funds; hold ≥1 year, ideally decades.

Japan

The new NISA: a decades-scale gift

Japan's reformed NISA (2024–) is one of the most generous retail tax wrappers anywhere. Inside it, capital gains and dividends are tax-free with no time limit, versus 20.315% tax in an ordinary account. For a resident of Japan compounding US or global index funds for 20–30 years, filling NISA first is close to a no-brainer.

Going deeper on NISA. The two quotas, how the ¥18M lifetime cap counts at book value, the misconceptions, and the administrative traps that quietly cost money — all of it is on the dedicated NISA page.

Japan resident priority order (a common default)

  1. Emergency fund in yen deposits.
  2. NISA tsumitate quota with a low-cost S&P 500 / all-country fund, automated monthly.
  3. iDeCo if the income deduction helps you and the age-60 lock is acceptable.
  4. NISA growth quota toward the ¥3.6M annual total.
  5. Tokutei account for anything beyond.

Japan's other tax breaks, beyond NISA (as of October 2026)

NISA makes investment gains tax-free. Japan also has several schemes that are deducted from income, so they cut this year's tax bill directly. Below are the rules in force now and the reforms that have already been enacted with fixed dates, ordered by how much they matter to a long-term investor.

iDeCo (individual defined-contribution pension): a major overhaul in December 2026

The tax relief comes in three layers. (1) Contributions are fully deductible from income, (2) gains are tax-free, and (3) payouts qualify for the retirement-income or public-pension deductions. For example, someone with a combined income and resident tax rate of 30% who contributes ¥23,000 a month (¥276,000 a year) gets back roughly ¥83,000 a year. The cost is that the money is locked until age 60.

  • When: the reform takes effect on 1 December 2026. The new ceilings apply from the December 2026 contribution, debited in January 2027. They do not rise automatically: you have to file a contribution change.
  • The ¥75,000 for Category 1 is shared with the National Pension Fund and the additional pension premium.
  • The ¥62,000 for employees and civil servants is shared with employer DC contributions and the equivalent value of a DB plan (before the reform the combined cap was ¥55,000, with iDeCo at most ¥20,000). The richer your employer plan, the less room is left for iDeCo.
  • Age: you can join or keep contributing until just before 70, unless you have already started drawing the basic old-age pension or iDeCo's old-age benefit.
  • Payouts: you choose a start between 60 and 75. A lump sum qualifies for the retirement-income deduction (¥400,000 per year of service up to 20 years, ¥700,000 per year beyond that).
  • Watch the payout order (from 2026): if you take the iDeCo lump sum first and a company retirement payment later, the look-back period over which the two deductions are netted has been extended from four prior years to nine. The old trick of spacing the two five years apart no longer works, so the order and gap between payouts need more planning.

Furusato nozei (hometown tax donations): gifts for a net ¥2,000, but the rules change every year

Donate to a municipality of your choice and the donation minus ¥2,000 is deducted from income and resident tax, up to a cap. Return gifts are limited to 30% of the donation. The cap depends on income and family; a rough guide is about 20% of your income-based resident tax. It is more accurate to see this as turning part of the tax you already owe into goods than as a way to pay less tax.

  • Filing: salaried workers who do not need a tax return and give to five or fewer municipalities in a year can use the one-stop exception (forms must arrive by 10 January of the following year). With six or more, or if you file a return for another reason such as the medical-expense deduction, claim the donation on the return; the one-stop forms then stop counting.
  • Calendar year: to count for this year, the payment must clear by 31 December.
  • Return gifts are occasional income: they become taxable if, together with other occasional income, they exceed the ¥500,000 annual special deduction.

The next NISA changes: from January 2027

  • Children's NISA (kodomo NISA): the tsumitate quota opens to ages 0–17. Up to ¥600,000 a year and ¥6 million in total (at cost), in the same funds as the tsumitate quota. Withdrawals are generally barred until 12; from 12 they are allowed with the child's consent and other conditions. At 18 the account rolls into the adult NISA and its ¥18 million lifetime limit.
  • Wider tsumitate menu: bond-focused funds and funds tracking the Yomiuri Stock Index and the JPX Prime 150 are set to become eligible.
  • The adult limits of ¥3.6 million a year and ¥18 million for life are unchanged.

Korea

연금저축, IRP, ISA — and the 22% overseas gains tax

Korean investors holding US-listed stocks directly face a distinctive tax landscape: capital gains on overseas-listed stocks are taxed at 22% (including local surtax) on net gains above a ₩2.5M annual deduction, self-reported each May. Losses realized the same year offset gains. US dividends are withheld at 15% under the treaty, and large financial income can trigger comprehensive taxation. The sheltered accounts below can hold Korean-listed ETFs that track US indexes — which is how most long-term Korean investors get tax-advantaged S&P 500 exposure.

Cross-border fine print

Three things non-US holders of US stocks should know

1. Dividend withholding

The US withholds tax on dividends before they reach you: 10% for Japanese residents, 15% for Korean residents under the respective treaties — make sure your broker has a valid W-8BEN on file, or the default rate is 30%. Your home country may tax the dividend again, with a foreign-tax credit at filing. Capital gains, by contrast, are generally not taxed by the US for treaty-country residents — only at home.

2. US estate tax

Non-resident aliens get only a $60,000 exemption on US-situs assets (including US-listed stocks) before US estate tax of up to 40% applies. Japan has an estate-tax treaty with the US that can substantially raise the effective exemption; Korea has none. For large portfolios this is a real planning issue — some non-US investors use non-US-domiciled funds (e.g. Ireland-listed UCITS) partly for this reason. Talk to a cross-border professional before it matters.

3. Currency & costs

FX conversion fees at purchase and sale are a real cost — often larger than fund fees — and vary widely between brokers. Home-listed funds that hold US stocks (a Tokyo-listed S&P 500 fund, a KRX-listed S&P 500 ETF) push FX handling inside the fund at institutional rates and fit sheltered accounts. See the currency section for the hedged-vs-unhedged question.
